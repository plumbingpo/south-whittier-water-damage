Appliance Leak Water Damage Help in South Whittier

Most of the appliance leaks we see trace back to three machines. When the supply hose on a washing machine splits, water flows under full pressure until somebody turns it off. A dishwasher tends to leak slowly from a door seal or drain hose, out of sight under the cabinets. The ice maker line on a refrigerator is a thin tube, and it can drip for months behind the unit. Each one needs a different kind of cleanup.

For homeowners in South Whittier, we begin by sliding the machine out to see how far the water traveled. In kitchens that usually means under the cabinets and beneath the flooring. Laundry room water goes into the wall at the back of the machine and the neighboring rooms, and with an upstairs laundry, the ceiling underneath. Then our team removes the standing water, meters the materials that soaked it up, and places equipment matched to the size of the damage.

First Five Minutes

Your First Steps

  1. Turn Off the ApplianceIf the outlet and the floor next to it are both dry, end the cycle and take the plug out of the wall. If not, switch it off at the breaker instead.
  2. Close the Supply ValveBehind a washer, on the wall, are separate hot and cold valves. Dishwashers shut off under the sink. The small valve for an ice maker line sits behind the refrigerator or underneath.
  3. Mop Up What You CanUse towels or a mop on whatever water is within reach, which keeps it from spreading beneath flooring and cabinets. Do not vacuum unless the machine is rated for wet and dry use.
  4. Leave the Door ShutIf the washer or dishwasher has not drained, leave it shut. When the door opens mid-cycle, the full load of water can pour out on the floor.
  5. Document What FailedPhotograph the failed fitting, valve or hose, and hold on to the part itself. Both the appliance maker and your insurer may want to look at it.
Coverage

Insurance and Appliance Leak Water Damage

When a machine overflows unexpectedly or a hose bursts, that is typically treated as sudden and accidental, which many homeowners policies cover. The policy typically pays for the water damage, not for the appliance or the failed part. Gradual leaks usually fall outside coverage, so slow seepage at a dishwasher or an ice maker line makes for a tougher claim. Some insurers also ask about the age and upkeep of the hose. Hold on to the part that failed, photograph everything ahead of cleanup, and review what your policy says.

Why It Happens

What Causes It

  • Plastic or copper ice maker lines that kink, crack or loosen at the fitting.
  • Poor installation, such as a dishwasher set out of level or a supply fitting not fully tightened.
  • A clogged defrost drain or cracked drain pan that lets a refrigerator leak onto the floor.
  • Washer supply hoses made of rubber that get weak over the years and burst from the pressure.
  • Standpipes that clog, or the drain hose from a washer coming loose from one.

Where the Damage Spreads

Kitchen Cabinets

Toe kicks and cabinet sides take on water from underneath when a dishwasher leaks. Panels swell and come apart, and a wet cabinet box can keep the wall behind it damp for weeks.

Flooring and Subfloor

A leak runs under laminate, hardwood or vinyl plank flooring with little resistance. Cupping and swelling show up in the boards, and the subfloor can stay damp long after the surface appears dry.

Baseboards and Wall Bottoms

On a flooded kitchen or laundry floor, the baseboards and the lower edge of the drywall draw water upward. Since it is out of view, the wall behind the machine tends to be wetter than the rest.

Rooms Next Door

For hours, a failed washer hose can keep releasing water. That water moves beneath walls into bedrooms, closets and hallways, soaking pad and carpet far from the laundry room.
